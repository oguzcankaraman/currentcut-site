How to keep help-center videos up to date

By Oğuzcan Karaman · Updated Nov 12, 2026

A tutorial video is accurate on the day it is published and starts drifting on the next release. Most teams do not have a video problem, they have a maintenance problem. Here is a process that keeps videos in sync with the product without a re-edit every sprint.

Why help-center videos go stale

Articles are text: changing a button name is a one-minute edit. A video bakes the old screen, the old voice-over and the old timing into one file, so a small UI change means re-recording, re-editing and re-uploading. That cost is why a 2021 video often sits next to an article that was edited last month.

The result is visible to users: they follow the video, the menu is not where it says, and they open a support ticket instead.

A five-step process

  1. Keep one task per video. "Create a survey from a template", not "Getting started with everything". Short, single-task videos (about 50 to 90 seconds) are the only kind you can afford to redo.
  2. Keep a list of every video and where it is embedded. A spreadsheet is enough: title, flow shown, articles that embed it, publish date, last checked date. You cannot refresh what you cannot find.
  3. Tie each video to a product area. When a release touches that area (check the changelog or release notes), the video is flagged for a check. This is the step teams skip, and it is what turns "update when we remember" into a routine.
  4. Script the flow so it can be replayed. Write the exact click path and the narration as text. When the UI changes you fix the affected steps and re-record, instead of improvising a new take.
  5. Re-record and replace in place. Keep the same embed or URL so articles, onboarding emails and in-app links keep working. Record the old and new version side by side once so you can see what changed.

What to check on each release

  • Menu and navigation names or positions that the video shows.
  • Button labels and screens in the click path.
  • Any step the video skips that is now required.
  • Plan or permission changes that hide a feature the video uses.
  • Narration that mentions a name, a number or a layout.

If a video fails any of these, replace it. A wrong video is worse than none, because the viewer trusts it.
